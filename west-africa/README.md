# West Africa study game

Study game for Sam, Lessons 11 and 12 of the social studies book: **Early Societies in West Africa** and **Ghana: A West African Trading Empire**.

Open it from the science game's home page, or go straight to `west-africa/` on GitHub Pages: https://brookhouser.github.io/sam-science-game/west-africa/

Modes: flashcards, quiz, matching, speed round, north-or-south trade goods, put in order, four vegetation zones, missed items, study guide, Test Prep, and Defend Kumbi (tower defense). Answering questions earns gold dust. Gold dust buys defenses that stop raiders on the caravan trail into Kumbi.

The teacher's study guide says the test is **three short answers** (at least three sentences each) and **one longer answer**, for the whole class period. Test Prep uses that format. The quiz is extra practice. It is not the shape of the test.

Every question below comes from the scanned textbook pages. Nothing was added from outside the scan.

- The study guide is a one-page scan. The printed test rules were clear. Two handwritten fill-ins were not: who "got travelers through the Atlas Mountains," and where Ibn Battuta "traveled to." ## Lesson 11 questions

### Word cards

- **artifact** — An item left behind by an earlier culture. Iron tools and iron-smelting furnaces are artifacts of the Nok. (p. 146)
- **Jenne-jeno** — An ancient West African city where the Niger River meets the Bani River. It was built in the third century B.C.E. and lasted more than 1,600 years. (p. 147)
- **Niger River** — The longest river in West Africa. In early times it was a kind of trading highway. People traveled it by canoe. (p. 144)
- **Nok** — A people who lived in what is now central Nigeria. By the 500s B.C.E. they made iron tools. (p. 146)
- **Sahara** — A large, hot desert in North Africa. It covers about 3.5 million square miles. (p. 144)
- **Sahel** — A zone of semidesert just south of the Sahara, where short grasses, small bushes, and a few trees grow. (p. 144)
- **savanna** — A vegetation zone of tall grasses and scattered trees. It has a long rainy season. (p. 144)
- **smelting** — The process of melting ore to extract iron or other metals. (p. 146)
- **tribute** — A payment made by one ruler or country to another for protection, or as a sign of submission. (p. 148)
- **extended family** — A farming community of close relatives, such as grandparents, aunts, uncles, and cousins. Early ones south of the Sahara had about 15 to 20 members. (p. 145)
- **blacksmith** — A skilled worker who hammered red-hot iron into tools and weapons. In Jenne-jeno, blacksmiths were the most respected people. (p. 146)
- **empire** — What a kingdom became when its king conquered more land. The king might send a governor to rule a conquered area, or let the people rule themselves. (p. 148)

### Questions

- **What key question does Lesson 11 ask?** Answer: What was the most significant factor in the development of early societies in West Africa? (p. 143)
- **What does Lesson 11 say played a critical role in the growth of West African societies?** Answer: Trade. The lesson says trade played a critical role in the growth and advancement of West African societies. (p. 144)
- **Why was the Sahara a poor place for large settlements?** Answer: The Sahara is very dry except for some water sources with a little vegetation, so it was not a good place for large settlements. (p. 144)
- **About how much of the Sahara is sand dunes?** Answer: Sand dunes cover about one-quarter of the Sahara. The rest includes bare rock, plains, and even mountains. (p. 144)
- **Which grains grow on the savanna?** Answer: The savanna has a long rainy season, so millet, sorghum, and rice can grow there. Grasses feed cattle, camels, goats, and sheep. (p. 144)
- **How did the Niger River help nearby communities?** Answer: The Niger River helped fertilize nearby land and provided fish. People also traveled it by canoe to trade. It was a kind of trading highway. (p. 144)
- **What is the forest zone in southern West Africa like?** Answer: The forest zone is wetter than the savanna. The northern part is a woodland of trees and shrubs. The southern part is rainforest, where rain falls year-round. Mahogany and teak rise above swamps and lagoons. (p. 144)
- **What are the borders of the West Africa region in this lesson?** Answer: West Africa begins in the Sahara on the north. The Atlantic Ocean borders it on the west and south. The mountains of present-day Cameroon border it on the east. (p. 144)
- **Why did people in different vegetation zones trade with each other?** Answer: Different resources are found in each vegetation zone. People traded for items they could not provide for themselves. People on the savanna may have traded grains for yams or mahogany from forest dwellers. (p. 145)
- **How did most early trade in West Africa move?** Answer: Several rivers were trade routes, and the Niger became a trading highway. People traveled by canoe. Some traders crossed the Sahara from North Africa, but most early trade was among settlements. (p. 145)
- **By about when had some people settled to farm south of the Sahara?** Answer: By about 4000 B.C.E. (p. 145)
- **Who lived in the earliest farming communities, and about how many people were in one?** Answer: The earliest farming communities were extended families: grandparents, aunts, uncles, and cousins, about 15 to 20 people. They produced most of what they needed and worked together to clear fields, plant, and harvest. (p. 145)
- **Who likely made decisions for an early family community?** Answer: Very likely, one of the male elders made decisions for the family community. (p. 145)
- **What happened when family communities joined together?** Answer: Family-based communities joined to form villages that might contain one- to two-hundred people. The village leader was probably chosen for his wisdom and strength. (p. 145)
- **Why did extended families band together in villages?** Answer: Extended families banded together to get needed help. They might have to deal with a flooding river or mine iron or gold. They also banded together for protection. (p. 146)
- **What ruins were found at the village of Dhar Tichitt, and what do they suggest?** Answer: Archaeologists found ruins of high walls and gates at Dhar Tichitt, in present-day Mauritania. These structures suggest that villagers united to defend themselves from outside attacks. (p. 146)
- **What two changes helped some West African villages grow into towns and cities?** Answer: The growth of ironworking and the expansion of trade (p. 146)
- **Who mastered ironworking as long ago as 1500 B.C.E.?** Answer: The Hittites of present-day Turkey mastered ironworking as long ago as 1500 B.C.E. The knowledge spread and eventually reached West Africa. (p. 146)
- **How might ironworking have reached West Africa?** Answer: Perhaps with traders who crossed the Sahara. Some scholars think it also grew on its own in northern West Africa. (p. 146)
- **What did the Nok make, and what artifacts have been found?** Answer: By the 500s B.C.E., the Nok, who lived in what is now central Nigeria, made iron tools. Important artifacts include their iron tools and iron-smelting furnaces. (p. 146)
- **What did Nok furnaces burn, and what did blacksmiths do with the iron?** Answer: Smelting is melting ore to get metal. The Nok used enormous amounts of charcoal to fuel their furnaces. Blacksmiths hammered and bent the red-hot iron into axes, hoes, and weapons such as spears. (p. 146)
- **Why did iron tools let villages grow?** Answer: With iron tools, farmers could clear land and cultivate crops more efficiently than with stone tools. More food supported larger villages, and more people were free to weave, work metal, and make pottery. (p. 146)
- **How could a village on a trade route become wealthy?** Answer: Villages on rivers or other easy routes became important trading sites. Villages that controlled trade routes became market centers. Their people grew wealthy by charging fees, and newcomers came to trade, learn crafts, and help build public buildings. (p. 146)
- **What old idea did the discovery of Jenne-jeno prove wrong?** Answer: In 1977, archaeologists began excavating Jenne-jeno. Until that rediscovery, some historians believed cities did not exist in West Africa until outsiders arrived and helped local people build them. Jenne-jeno proved that theory wrong. (p. 147)
- **When did archaeologists begin excavating Jenne-jeno?** Answer: In 1977, archaeologists began excavating the ancient West African city of Jenne-jeno. Until that rediscovery, some historians believed cities did not exist in West Africa until outsiders arrived. (p. 147)
- **What is special about Jenne-jeno?** Answer: Jenne-jeno is special because, in 1977, archaeologists began excavating it and showed that a West African city existed before outsiders arrived to build one. It was built in the third century B.C.E. where the Niger meets the Bani, lasted more than 1,600 years, and grew to about 20,000 people inside a wall 10 feet wide and 13 feet high. The people prized iron more than gold. Blacksmiths were the most respected people. Many thought blacksmiths had supernatural powers, and blacksmiths could act as political leaders, judges, and doctors. (p. 147)
- **Why was Jenne-jeno built where it was?** Answer: It stood where the Niger River meets the Bani River, a strong place for farming, fishing, and trade. (p. 147)
- **What did the people of Jenne-jeno trade?** Answer: They traded surplus goods, such as catfish, onions, and rice, for salt, iron ore, copper, and gold. The copper came from 600 miles away. (p. 147)
- **How many people lived in Jenne-jeno, and what was the wall like?** Answer: Jenne-jeno grew into a busy city of about 20,000 people. It was surrounded by a wall 10 feet wide and 13 feet high. The wall may have been built to give the city more status and to control the coming and going of traders. (p. 147)
- **What were the houses in Jenne-jeno like?** Answer: People lived in circular houses. At first they built them from bent poles and woven mats. Later they used mud blocks. (p. 147)
- **What crafts were practiced in Jenne-jeno?** Answer: Besides farmers and fishers, there were potters, metalsmiths, weavers, leatherworkers, bead makers, and ivory carvers. (p. 147)
- **Why were blacksmiths the most respected people in Jenne-jeno?** Answer: West Africans prized iron more than gold. They were amazed that blacksmiths could make tools from iron. As in many early cultures, people thought blacksmiths had supernatural, or magical or godlike, powers. Blacksmiths could act as political leaders, judges, and doctors. (p. 147)
- **How close is ancient Jenne-jeno to the modern city of Jenne?** Answer: Jenne-jeno's location on the Niger was less than two miles from the modern city of Jenne. (p. 147)
- **Which three kingdoms arose in West Africa between 500 and 1600 C.E.?** Answer: Between 500 and 1600 C.E., three kingdoms arose in West Africa: Ghana, Mali, and Songhai. (p. 143)
- **How did trade help the first West African kingdoms rise?** Answer: Ghana, Mali, and Songhai were trading powers. A city could grow wealthy by controlling trade. With that wealth its ruler could raise a large army, conquer nearby trading areas, take over their trade, and collect tribute. (p. 148)
- **What two kinds of leaders were West African kings?** Answer: West African kings were both political and religious leaders. People believed the gods gave them special powers. The kings performed religious ceremonies to please the gods. (p. 148)
- **What is one disadvantage and one advantage of being conquered and brought into a kingdom?** Answer: Disadvantages included paying tribute and serving in the king's army. Advantages: the king provided protection, armies kept trade routes safe from raiders and foreign armies, wars between small cities ended, and the king gave expensive presents to his governors. (p. 148)
- **What does Lesson 11's summary say about geography?** Answer: The summary says geography was a major factor in the development of West African societies. Settlements flourished south of the Sahara, where the land permitted farming. Communities traded for items they could not produce locally. Rivers such as the Niger served as trade routes. (p. 149)
- **What city does the caravan picture at the start of Lesson 11 show?** Answer: The caption says that behind the caravan is Timbuktu, a city in the early West African kingdom of Mali. (p. 143)
- **What did the map of early kingdoms say they all relied on?** Answer: The great kingdoms did not rely only on local trade. By the time Ghana was a significant power, trans-Saharan trade was bringing new wealth from other regions, such as the present-day Middle East. Control of trade, particularly in West African gold, was also crucial to the power of Mali. Songhai, too, relied on trade with distant lands. (p. 149)

### Put in order

  1. By about 4000 B.C.E., extended families farmed south of the Sahara.
  2. Families joined into villages of about 100 to 200 people for work and protection.
  3. Iron tools helped farmers grow more food, so villages could get larger.
  4. Villages traded surplus food and crafts. Those on trade routes became market centers.
  5. Some market centers grew into towns and cities, such as Jenne-jeno.
  6. Rulers of rich cities raised armies, conquered neighbors, collected tribute, and built kingdoms.
- **How ironworking spread** (p. 146)
  1. The Hittites in present-day Turkey mastered ironworking by about 1500 B.C.E.
  2. The knowledge spread, perhaps with traders across the Sahara. It may also have grown on its own in northern West Africa.
  3. By the 500s B.C.E., the Nok of central Nigeria made iron tools and used smelting furnaces.
  4. Blacksmiths shaped red-hot iron into axes, hoes, and spears.
  5. Ironworking spread quickly, and iron tools replaced stone tools for farming.

## Lesson 12 questions

### Word cards

- **Ghana** — A medieval kingdom and empire in West Africa. It lasted from sometime before 500 C.E. until it finally collapsed in the 1200s. The word ghana means "war chief." (p. 151)
- **matrilineal** — A family line traced through the mother. In Ghana the king's own son did not inherit the throne. The son of the king's sister did. (p. 152)
- **trans-Saharan trade** — Trade between peoples north and south of the Sahara. (p. 154)
- **Wangara** — The secret gold country south of Ghana. The Wangarans never revealed where their mines were. (p. 156)
- **Taghaza** — A Sahara village where salt was mined. Traders stopped there to load blocks of salt. (p. 156)
- **Kumbi** — The capital of Ghana. It had the busiest market in West Africa. (p. 158)
- **silent barter** — Trading without speaking or meeting. Wangarans and caravan traders left goods and gold dust by a river and signaled with drums. (p. 158)
- **Almoravids** — Muslim warriors who attacked Ghana in the second half of the 11th century and captured Kumbi. (p. 159)
- **Mande** — A people whose homeland, Mali, lay south of Kumbi, closer to the Niger River. Around 1240 they conquered Kumbi and built a new empire. (p. 159)
- **Ibn Battuta** — A Muslim historian and traveler. In 1352 he crossed the Sahara with a trade caravan and wrote about the journey. (p. 155)
- **camel** — An animal Arab traders brought to the Sahara around 300 C.E. A camel can drink up to 25 gallons of water at a time, so it can walk for days in the desert. (p. 154)
- **cowrie shell** — A kind of seashell from North Africa. Traders used cowrie shells as money. (p. 154)
- **porter** — A person who carries goods for a living. At Walata, traders moved salt and other goods from camels onto donkeys and porters. (p. 155)

### Questions

- **What key question does Lesson 12 ask?** Answer: To what extent did trans-Saharan trade lead to Ghana's wealth and success? The lesson shows that controlling and taxing this trade, especially in gold and salt, made Ghana rich and powerful. War and the loss of natural resources later brought Ghana down. (p. 151)
- **When did the kingdom of Ghana last?** Answer: From sometime before 500 C.E. until its final collapse in the 1200s (p. 151)
- **Where did the kingdom of Ghana arise?** Answer: Ghana arose in the semidesert Sahel and spread over the valley between the Senegal and Niger rivers. Forest lay to the south and the Sahara to the north. Today this region is part of the modern nations of Mali and Mauritania. The modern country of Ghana, far to the south, takes its name from the old kingdom but is not in the same place. (p. 151)
- **What does the word ghana mean?** Answer: War chief (p. 151)
- **What do archaeologists conclude made Ghana's king and people wealthy?** Answer: Historians are not sure exactly how Ghana became an empire. Possibly warriors used iron weapons to defeat their neighbors. Archaeologists have concluded that control of trade, particularly the gold trade, made the king and his people very wealthy. (p. 151)
- **Who wrote the earliest accounts of the kingdom of Ghana?** Answer: The earliest writings about Ghana come from Arab scholars. They recorded what travelers told them. By the 9th century, when they began writing, Ghana was already a flourishing empire. (p. 151)
- **What jobs did Ghana's king hold?** Answer: The king was the head of the army, made the final decisions in matters of justice, and led the people in religious worship. (p. 152)
- **How did Ghana's king keep control of the gold supply?** Answer: To preserve his wealth, the king tightly controlled the supply of gold. All the gold nuggets found in the kingdom had to be given to him. Ordinary people could have only gold dust. (p. 152)
- **What legends were told about the king's gold nuggets?** Answer: One of the king's gold nuggets is said to have weighed almost forty pounds. According to legend, another was large enough to be used as a hitching post for his horse. (p. 152)
- **How did people greet the king at court?** Answer: Each day the king held court, dressed in colorful robes, gold jewelry, and a cap decorated with gold. He arrived to the beating of royal drums. People showed respect by kneeling and throwing dust on their heads. (p. 152)
- **Who inherited the throne when Ghana's king died?** Answer: The king's son did not inherit the throne. Royal inheritance was matrilineal, traced through women rather than men. The son of the king's sister was the heir. (p. 152)
- **What were the three parts of Ghana's military?** Answer: Ghana's military included a regular army of career soldiers, extra reserve forces called up in wartime, and elite soldiers chosen for courage, honesty, and intelligence. (p. 153)
- **What did Ghana's regular soldiers do, and how were they treated?** Answer: The regular army kept the borders secure, suppressed minor revolts, and maintained peace and order. These soldiers were well paid and highly respected. Every man in the empire had to complete military training so he could serve when called. (p. 153)
- **What weapons did Ghana's soldiers use?** Answer: Soldiers used spears, daggers, swords, battle clubs, and bows and arrows. They wore knee-length cotton pants, sleeveless tunics, sandals, and headdresses with feathers. The color of the tunic and the number of feathers showed rank. (p. 153)
- **What should you remember about the story of 200,000 warriors?** Answer: Stories describe a king who could call up 200,000 warriors. That number no doubt grew as the story was passed on, but the king certainly could summon a sizable army, including infantry archers. (p. 153)
- **What was the job of Ghana's elite soldiers?** Answer: Special groups were chosen for courage, honesty, and intelligence. These soldiers served as bodyguards, escorts, and military advisors. (p. 153)
- **What did Ghana do with their military?** Answer: Ghana's military had a regular army, reserve forces, and elite soldiers. The regular army guarded the borders, put down small revolts, and kept order. Soldiers wore knee-length cotton pants, sleeveless tunics, sandals, and headdresses with feathers. Tunic color and the number of feathers showed rank. Weapons included spears, daggers, swords, battle clubs, and bows and arrows. Elite soldiers were chosen for courage, honesty, and intelligence, and served as bodyguards, escorts, and advisors. Every man had to train. Stories say one king could call up 200,000 warriors. The book says that number grew as the story was passed on, but he could summon a large army, including archers on foot. Armies also kept trade routes safe from raiders. (p. 153)
- **How did Ghana get rich?** Answer: Ghana got rich by controlling trans-Saharan trade, especially gold, and by taxing goods that passed through. Gold came from secret mines in the forests to the south, in Wangara. Salt came from the Sahara to the north, especially Taghaza. Ghana did not have its own salt. It charged one-sixth of an ounce of gold for each load of salt entering from the north, and one-third of an ounce for each load going south. The king kept gold nuggets and let ordinary people keep only gold dust. Ghana arose in the semidesert Sahel. The book does not say good farmland made Ghana rich. It says Ghana's location let it control the trade, and that wealth paid for a strong army. (pp. 151, 154, 156, and 157)
- **What goods did traders from North Africa bring across the Sahara?** Answer: Traders from North Africa crossed the Sahara with salt, copper, and cowrie shells, a type of seashell used as money. (p. 154)
- **What did those traders take from the southern forests?** Answer: Merchants traded their goods for kola nuts, hides, leather goods, ivory, slaves, and gold from the southern part of West Africa. Then they carried those goods back to markets in North Africa. (p. 154)
- **Why did Ghana's location make it rich?** Answer: Ghana lay between North Africa and West Africa, so it could control trans-Saharan trade. Traders paid heavy taxes on their goods each time they passed through. Those taxes helped make Ghana rich. (p. 154)
- **How early did North Africans bring gold out of the southern forests?** Answer: Trans-Saharan trade has a long history. Archaeologists have found evidence that North Africans brought gold back from the southern forests as long ago as 400 to 500 B.C.E. Travel across the Sahara was especially challenging for these early people. (p. 154)
- **What two changes helped trans-Saharan trade grow?** Answer: The introduction of the camel, and the spread of Islam (p. 154)
- **Why could camels cross the Sahara?** Answer: Arab traders first brought camels to the Sahara around 300 C.E. A camel can drink up to 25 gallons of water at a time and travel several days without stopping. Double rows of eyelashes and hairy ear openings help keep out blowing sand. (p. 154)
- **What was happening to West African gold by the 4th century C.E.?** Answer: Camels let traders open caravan routes. By the 4th century C.E., large amounts of gold were being made into Roman coins in North Africa. It is likely that gold came from West Africa. (p. 154)
- **What happened when Muslims invaded Ghana in the 7th century?** Answer: In the 7th century, Muslims invaded Ghana's empire. They wanted to convert West Africans to Islam and to control trade. Ghana turned back the invaders, but many Muslims settled in West African towns and became merchants. Trade kept expanding because of the spread of Islam. (p. 154)
- **What was true of Ghana's power by the year 1000?** Answer: By the year 1000, Ghana's empire dominated the trade routes between North and West Africa. The empire reached its height around the year 1000. (p. 154)
- **Who crossed the Sahara in 1352, and why does his writing matter?** Answer: In 1352 Ibn Battuta crossed the Sahara with a trade caravan. He was a Muslim historian and traveler, and his account describes the journey. (p. 155)
- **Where did Battuta's caravan begin its desert crossing?** Answer: The caravan started at the oasis city of Sijilmasa. Donkeys had carried goods from the Mediterranean coast, from Europe, Arabia, and Egypt, to Sijilmasa. Then camels took the goods south. (p. 155)
- **How did the caravan cross the desert day by day?** Answer: They stayed in Sijilmasa until the rainy season ended, when watering places were full and there was grass for the animals. Each day the traders walked until the afternoon, when the sun was high, and then rested until sunset. (p. 155)
- **What danger does Battuta describe in the desert?** Answer: Walking across the Sahara was hard and dangerous. Caravans sometimes lost their way, and some traders died. During one stretch they could not find water, so they slaughtered some camels and drank the water stored in the animals' stomachs. (p. 155)
- **What did the caravan do at Taghaza and at Walata?** Answer: On the way through the desert the caravan stopped at Taghaza, where the salt mines were, and took on a load of salt. At Walata, on the edge of the desert, they transferred the salt and other goods from camels to donkeys and to porters, people who carry goods for a living. (p. 155)
- **How long did the full trip to the markets near the Gulf of Guinea take?** Answer: From Sijilmasa the routes stretched across the desert, through Ghana, south to the Gulf of Guinea, and east toward present-day Chad. The entire journey took about two months. (p. 155)
- **Why did people south of the desert crave salt?** Answer: Salt is important in the diet because people and animals lose salt when they sweat. People in hot West Africa had to replace it. They also needed salt to keep food from spoiling, to give to their cattle, and because they liked the taste. For many of them, salt was more precious than gold. They had little use for gold except as something to trade. (p. 156)
- **Where did West Africans get salt, and how was it produced?** Answer: West Africans had no local source of salt, so they got it from Taghaza and other places in the Sahara. One method: water was poured into holes in salty earth, drew out the salt, and evaporated in the sun. The salt was packed into blocks. The other method was mining. (p. 156)
- **How was salt mined at Taghaza?** Answer: At Taghaza, salt deposits were below the surface. Workers enslaved by Arab merchants reached the salt by digging tunnels, then cut it out in large blocks. (p. 156)
- **What were Taghaza's houses built from, and how did people there get food?** Answer: Even the houses in Taghaza were built of salt blocks. Because no food was produced there, people relied on caravans to bring food, such as dates and millet. If the caravans did not arrive, the miners starved. (p. 157)
- **What salt tax did Ghana charge?** Answer: Traders paid taxes on goods both when they entered Ghana and when they left. Ghana charged one-sixth of an ounce of gold for each load of salt that came in from the north, and one-third of an ounce of gold for each load taken south. (p. 157)
- **What did Ghana charge on copper and on general merchandise?** Answer: For every load of copper, traders were charged five-eighths of an ounce of gold. They paid slightly more than one ounce of gold per load of general merchandise. (p. 157)
- **What did Ghana's tax money pay for?** Answer: The taxes enriched Ghana's treasury and helped pay for armies. Those armies protected the kingdom, let the king conquer other territory, and guarded trade routes against raiders who might rob the caravans. (p. 157)
- **How did the Wangarans protect the secret of their gold mines?** Answer: The Wangarans kept the location of their gold mines secret. Stories say merchants sometimes captured a miner and tried to force him to reveal Wangara. The miners would give their lives rather than tell. In one tale, after a miner was captured, the Wangarans stopped trading for three years so no one would discover the way to Wangara. To this day, no one knows for certain exactly where the mines were. (p. 156)
- **What was Kumbi's market like?** Answer: Caravans brought goods to the great market in Kumbi, then many went on to trade with the Wangarans. Ironsmiths sold weapons and tools, goldsmiths and coppersmiths sold jewelry, weavers sold cloth, and leatherworkers sold leather goods. Shoppers could also find blue blouses from Spain, robes from Morocco, cattle, sheep, honey, wheat, raisins, dried fruit, ivory, pearls, and slaves. All goods were paid for with gold dust. (p. 158)
- **What does the lesson say about Kumbi's slave market?** Answer: Kumbi had one of the largest slave markets in West Africa. Raiders captured people along the southern border of Ghana. Many were bought at Kumbi by Arab merchants, who took them across the Sahara and sold them to North Africans or Europeans. (p. 158)
- **What goods did caravans lay out for the Wangarans?** Answer: Trade with the Wangarans happened along a river in the southern forests. The caravans brought wool, silk, cotton, dates, figs, grains, leather, and salt. (p. 158)
- **How did silent barter work?** Answer: Traders spread their goods along the river, beat a drum to announce an offer, and walked several miles away. When the Wangarans heard the drum, they came by boat, put some gold dust next to the goods, beat a drum, and left. If the gold was enough, the traders took it. If not, they waited for the Wangarans to leave more. They bargained back and forth without ever meeting. (p. 158)
- **What were two advantages of silent barter?** Answer: Silent barter had two advantages. First, people who spoke different languages could trade. Second, it let the Wangarans protect the secret location of their gold mines. (p. 158)
- **What did the Almoravids do to Ghana?** Answer: Ghana reached its height around the year 1000. In the second half of the 11th century, Muslim warriors called the Almoravids began attacking and captured Kumbi. Ghana's king regained the capital, but the old empire had already broken apart. (p. 159)
- **How did the loss of trees and water weaken Ghana?** Answer: The loss of natural resources weakened Ghana further. A growing population put great stress on scarce resources such as trees and water. Trees were cut down to provide charcoal for iron-smelting furnaces. Water became so scarce that farmers could no longer grow crops or keep flocks. People left to search for better conditions. (p. 159)
- **When did Ghana's empire come to an end?** Answer: In 1203, when a rival kingdom conquered Kumbi (p. 159)
- **How did the empire of Mali begin?** Answer: Around 1240 a group of West Africans called the Mande conquered Kumbi. Their homeland of Mali was south of Kumbi, closer to the Niger River. They built an empire that reached from the Atlantic Ocean to beyond the Niger River, and from the southern forests to the salt and copper mines of the Sahara. (p. 159)
- **How was Mali's wealth like Ghana's?** Answer: Like Ghana, Mali gained much of its wealth from control of trade, particularly gold. Its leaders had accepted Islam, and under their rule Islam became even more influential in West Africa. (p. 159)
- **What does the Lesson 12 summary say brought Ghana down?** Answer: The summary says years of war and the loss of natural resources led to Ghana's downfall in the 13th century. The next powerful West African empire, Mali, also built its wealth on trade. (p. 159)
- **What was the horse-and-rider brass weight used for?** Answer: A brass weight shaped like a horse and rider was made by the Ashanti people of Ghana. Trans-Saharan traders used this artifact to weigh gold dust. (p. 154)

### Put in order

- **How trans-Saharan trade grew** (p. 154)
  1. As early as 400 to 500 B.C.E., North Africans brought gold from the southern forests, but the desert crossing was very hard.
  2. Around 300 C.E., Arab traders brought camels to the Sahara.
  3. Camels made caravan routes possible. By the 4th century C.E., West African gold was likely being made into Roman coins.
  4. In the 7th century, Islam spread. Ghana turned back an invasion, and many Muslims stayed as merchants.
  5. By the year 1000, Ghana controlled the trade routes between North Africa and West Africa.
- **Ibn Battuta's journey south in 1352** (p. 155)
  1. Donkeys carried goods from the Mediterranean coast to the oasis city of Sijilmasa.
  2. After the rainy season, camel caravans headed south from oasis to oasis.
  3. Each day the travelers walked until afternoon, then rested until sunset.
  4. At Taghaza they loaded salt from the mines.
  5. At Walata they moved the goods from camels to donkeys and porters.
  6. They continued through Ghana toward markets near the Gulf of Guinea. The trip took about two months.
- **Steps of silent barter with the Wangarans** (p. 158)
  1. Caravans spread wool, silk, cotton, dates, figs, grains, leather, and salt along a river.
  2. The traders beat a drum and walked several miles away.
  3. Wangarans came by boat, left some gold dust, beat a drum, and left.
  4. If the gold was enough, the traders took it. If not, they waited while the Wangarans added more.
  5. The two groups bargained this way without ever meeting in person.
- **Ghana's rise and Mali's rise** (p. 159)
  1. Sometime before 500 C.E., Ghana was underway in the Sahel and grew rich by controlling trade, especially gold.
  2. Around the year 1000, Ghana dominated the trans-Saharan routes and the empire was at its height.
  3. In the second half of the 11th century, the Almoravids captured Kumbi. The king won it back, but the empire had broken apart.
  4. Loss of trees and water, plus war, weakened Ghana. In 1203 a rival kingdom conquered Kumbi.
  5. Around 1240 the Mande conquered Kumbi and built the empire of Mali, which also grew rich from trade.
- **One way salt was collected in the Sahara** (p. 156)
  1. Water was poured into holes in the salty earth.
  2. The water drew the salt out of the ground.
  3. The water evaporated in the sun.
  4. The salt that remained was packed into blocks.

### North or south

- **Salt** — north, across the Sahara. Salt came from Taghaza and other places in the Sahara and was carried south. (p. 156)
- **Copper** — north, across the Sahara. Traders from North Africa brought copper across the Sahara. (p. 154)
- **Cowrie shells** — north, across the Sahara. Cowrie shells were seashells from North Africa, used as money. (p. 154)
- **Dates** — north, across the Sahara. Caravans brought dates south to trade, including with the Wangarans. (p. 158)
- **Gold** — south, the forests of West Africa. Gold came from secret mines in Wangara, in the forests south of Ghana. (p. 156)
- **Kola nuts** — south, the forests of West Africa. Traders picked up kola nuts in the southern part of West Africa. (p. 154)
- **Ivory** — south, the forests of West Africa. Ivory was one of the goods from the southern forests. (p. 154)
- **Hides** — south, the forests of West Africa. Hides were traded from the southern part of West Africa. (p. 154)

## Homework practice questions

These three questions are in flashcards, the quiz, and Test Prep. The short answers on the practice sheet looked like a student's notes. Where they disagree with the textbook, the game uses the textbook.

Checked against the book:

- **Ghana selected people who were smart, honest, and brave.** Close, but only part of the military. Page 153 says the *elite* soldiers were chosen for courage, honesty, and intelligence. They were bodyguards, escorts, and advisors. The regular army were career soldiers who guarded the borders, put down revolts, and kept order. Every man trained.
- **Soldiers wore cotton pants down to their knees, sleeveless shirts, sandals, and feathers.** Close. The book says knee-length cotton pants, sleeveless tunics, sandals, and headdresses adorned with feathers. Tunic color and the number of feathers showed rank.
- **The Ghana military had 200,000 people.** Only partly right. Stories describe a king who could call up 200,000 warriors, and the study-guide fill-in uses that number. The same page says the number grew as the story was passed on. He could still summon a sizable army, including archers on foot.
- **Ghana traded a lot of gold.** True, and incomplete. The book says control of trade, especially gold, plus taxes. The king kept the nuggets. People could have only gold dust.
- **Ghana had lots of salt.** The textbook contradicts this. Salt came from Taghaza and other places in the Sahara, to the north. People south of the desert had no local salt. Ghana taxed salt passing through: one-sixth of an ounce of gold per load entering from the north, and one-third of an ounce per load going south.
- **Ghana had good land.** The textbook contradicts this as the reason Ghana got rich. Ghana arose in the semidesert Sahel. Its wealth was its location and the taxes on trans-Saharan trade, which paid for the army. Farming land in this lesson is the savanna south of the Sahel, not the explanation of Ghana's wealth.
- **No one knew about Jenne-jeno until 1977.** The year is in the book. Page 147 says archaeologists began excavating in 1977. "No one knew" is stronger than the book. Until that rediscovery, some historians believed West African cities did not exist until outsiders arrived and helped build them. Jenne-jeno proved that wrong.
- **They would praise iron more than gold.** Supported. The book says West Africans prized iron more than gold.
- **Blacksmiths were amazing back then.** Not contradicted, but too vague for the specifics the study guide wants. The book says blacksmiths were the most respected people in Jenne-jeno. People thought they had supernatural powers, and they could act as political leaders, judges, and doctors.
